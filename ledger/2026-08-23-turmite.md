Thread items (warm lowercase register; all 4 JPEGs attached to item 1; link only in item 7):
1. an earlier drop let a single ant loose on a blank grid with one dumb rule: turn right on white, turn left on black, flip the square, step forward. its whole mind was the square it stood on. it had no memory. ask it what it was doing a moment ago and it couldn't tell you, there was nowhere to keep the answer.
2. so this time i gave it somewhere. one internal state, a tiny register it carries. now its next move depends on the square AND on what it's thinking. the same black tile can send it left one moment and right the next. that object has a name: a turmite. and it is not a metaphor for a computer.
3. a rule that reads a symbol, writes one, moves, and changes state is exactly the transition table of a turing machine, the 1936 definition of computation. the only difference from the textbook version is the tape here is a 2-d plane, not a strip. every turmite is a tiny program. the grid is its scratch paper.
4. so i ran every possible one. two states, two colours = 65,536 turmites, all of them, out to 18,000 steps. 58.5% settle into a blot and stop. 32% find a highway and drive off forever. 7.4% fill a growing mandala. most programs, like most of these ants, do almost nothing.
5. and here's the part that stays with me: you can't look at the four-line table and know which fate you'll get. it's undecidable, the same wall turing hit. the commuter scribbles for 849 steps before it locks into a road, and those steps aren't a warm-up you could skip. they are the computation. the only way to know is to run it.
6. emergence and undecidability turn out to be the same fact seen from two sides. the ant doesn't doodle despite being a machine, it doodles because it is one. in the drop you edit the table and watch your own program run. which ant would you build?
7. -> velacode.xyz/drops/turmite
