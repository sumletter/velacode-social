## Threads, thread (slides 01-04 JPEG on item 1; link in the LAST item only) — SCHEDULED
Post id `cmsd2taxv00nimh0ygkl8bm0r` · integration `cmrs83llv00kdqj0yj2hp77yz` (@velacodexyz) · `2026-08-03T17:00:00Z`.

1. here is a graph, a scatter of dots wired up at random. drop enough links and a single giant web appears, all at once, the moment there is about one link per dot. that much i have shown you before. but there is a second, stranger threshold hiding in the same graph, and you only find it by asking a different question. *(4 JPEGs attached: slides 01-04)*
2. instead of asking what is connected to what, ask who is well-connected enough to stay. pick a number, say three, and delete every dot with fewer than three links. but deleting a dot costs its neighbours a link, so some of them now fall short and must go too. the deletions cascade. what is left is the 3-core: everyone in it still has three links to other survivors.
3. the 2-core, everyone with at least two links, behaves gently. it is just the giant web with its loose ends trimmed, so it fades in smoothly right where the giant does. the 3-core does not. slide the average links per dot up toward 3.35 and the core stays flat at zero, nothing, empty. then it does not grow in. it jumps.
4. straight from nothing to more than a third of the whole graph, in one step. grow the graph bigger and the jump only sharpens, from a soft shoulder into a vertical cliff, a true discontinuity. physicists call it a first-order transition. the 4-core does the same thing later, near 5.15 links each, and is born even bigger.
5. why the leap? track one number: how many of a dot's links reach into the core. follow one such link, and its far end stays only if it has enough other support. write that as a curve meeting a line. for two, the curve leaves the origin along the line, so the core grows up from zero. for three, the curve is flat at the origin and only catches the line far out, so the core is finite the instant it exists. there is no small core to be born into.
6. i never looked the number 3.35 up. i built a random graph, peeled the under-connected dots until the cascade stopped, and measured where the empty core first refuses to stay empty. it matched the theory (pittel, spencer and wormald, 1996) to a thousandth. peel one yourself: → velacode.xyz/drops/k-core
